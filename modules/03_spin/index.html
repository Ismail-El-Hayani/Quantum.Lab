📖 Theory — Why is spin quantized?

Unlike orbital angular momentum which comes from spatial motion, spin is an intrinsic property of the electron — it has no classical analogue. The Stern-Gerlach experiment (1922) proved it is quantized: a beam of silver atoms splits into exactly two paths, never three, never a continuum.

Pauli matrices:

$$\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Spin operator: $\vec{S} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\vec{\sigma}$

Any pure state:

$$|\psi\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|\uparrow_z\rangle + e^{i\phi}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}|\downarrow_z\rangle$$

Expectation value: $\langle\vec{S}\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}(\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$ — this is the arrow on the Bloch sphere.

Measurement Postulate
When you measure Sz, the state collapses to either |↑z⟩ or |↓z⟩ with probabilities |cos(θ/2)|² and |sin(θ/2)|². After the measurement, the state is the result — not a mixture. This is why you cannot measure Sx and Sz simultaneously: measuring one destroys information about the other.

The commutation relation $[S_x, S_y] = i\hbar S_z$ (and cyclic permutations) is the mathematical root of uncertainty. It means the spin operators do not share eigenstates — there is no state that is simultaneously an eigenstate of Sx and Sz.
