📖 The Story — From Isolated Atoms to Solid Bands

Imagine a single atom. Its electron energies are sharp, discrete levels. Now bring two atoms close together: each level splits into two. Bring in ten, a hundred, ten billion of them, arranged in a perfect lattice, and the discrete lines blur into broad energy bands separated by bandgaps. This is the Kronig-Penney model in words — the simplest picture of why solids are conductors, semiconductors, or insulators.

🧩 Historical Context

In 1931 Kronig and Penney proposed a one-dimensional periodic square-well potential to explain how electrons behave in a crystal. It became the textbook bridge between the free-electron model of metals and the tight-binding picture of atoms glued together by shared electrons. Bloch's theorem, proven in 1928, gave the rigorous reason: in a periodic potential, wavefunctions are modulated plane waves.

🔬 Why It Surprised Physicists

A free electron can have any energy. Put it in a periodic lattice and something strange happens: certain energies simply disappear. The allowed states collect into bands, and between them are forbidden gaps. This is not a property of the electron alone; it is a collective effect of the lattice. It is the origin of the semiconductor revolution: silicon's 1.1 eV gap is what makes transistors possible.

🎓 Theory — Bands from a Periodic Potential

The Kronig-Penney model replaces a real crystal with a one-dimensional periodic square-well potential: barriers of height V₀ and width b alternate with wells of width a − b. The period is a. An electron with energy E sees different wave numbers in the well (α) and in the barrier (β or β′), and the matching conditions across each cell lead to a transcendental equation for the allowed quasimomentum k.

1. The Kronig-Penney Transcendental Equation

For E < V₀ the right-hand side is:

$$\cos(ka) = \cos(\alpha w)\cosh(\beta b) + \frac{\beta^2 - \alpha^2}{2\alpha\beta}\sin(\alpha w)\sinh(\beta b)$$

where \(\alpha = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\), \(\beta = \sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar\), and \(w = a-b\). For E > V₀ replace cosh/sinh with cos/sin and change the sign of the mixing term.

2. Bloch's Theorem

Because the potential is periodic, every eigenstate can be written as a plane wave times a cell-periodic function:

$$\psi_k(x) = e^{ikx} u_k(x), \qquad u_k(x+a) = u_k(x)$$

The crystal momentum k is defined modulo the reciprocal lattice: k and k + 2π/a label the same state. This is why the bands can be folded into the first Brillouin zone [−π/a, +π/a] in the reduced-zone scheme.

3. Bandgaps at Bragg Planes

Gaps open whenever the free-electron parabola would cross a Bragg reflection condition. In 1D this happens at:

$$k = \frac{n\pi}{a}, \qquad n = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots$$

At these points two waves traveling in opposite directions Bragg-reflect and mix, splitting the degeneracy and opening a gap proportional to the Fourier component of the potential.

4. Effective Mass

Near a band minimum the dispersion is approximately parabolic. Comparing E(k) ≈ E₀ + ℏ²k²/(2m*) with the curvature of the computed band gives:

$$m^* = \hbar^2 \left(\frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial k^2}\right)^{-1}$$

The live readout reports m*/m₀ at the valence-band top and conduction-band bottom. Narrow bands have small curvature → large effective mass; wide bands have light effective mass.

5. Tunneling and the Origin of Bands

The single-barrier tunneling view shows an electron leaking through a classically forbidden region. In a periodic lattice this leakage is repeated every cell. Strong leakage (low V₀, thin b) makes atomic orbitals overlap → broad bands. Weak leakage isolates the atoms → narrow bands that look like discrete levels. The gaps are energies where the repeated reflections interfere destructively and no traveling state can exist.

✨ From Single Barrier to Bloch Bands

The Tunneling Simulation shows a single electron encountering one barrier. When E < V₀, the wave function decays exponentially inside the barrier; if the barrier is thin (small b/a), a non-zero amplitude leaks through to the far side. This is quantum mechanical tunneling — classically impossible, yet measurable in every semiconductor device.

The Band Structure is the global consequence of this leakage repeated across a periodic lattice. High tunneling (thin barriers, low V₀) makes atomic orbitals overlap strongly → broad energy bands. Low tunneling (thick barriers, high V₀) isolates the atoms → narrow bands that behave like discrete atomic levels. The gaps between bands are forbidden energies where no traveling wave can exist in the crystal.
