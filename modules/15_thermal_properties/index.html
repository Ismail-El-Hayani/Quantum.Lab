Thermal Properties

Explore how phonons and electrons store heat, conduct it, and make the lattice expand.

🔥 The Heat Chronicles

You arrive at the Thermal Observatory, a station suspended above a crystal lattice frozen at millikelvin temperatures. Your mission: understand why some solids conduct heat like lightning while others trap it like a cage, and why every crystal whispers a phonon song that changes pitch as it warms.

🧊 Phase I — Frozen Whispers

At cryogenic temperatures the lattice barely vibrates. Only long-wavelength acoustic phonons are excited. In insulators, heat capacity follows Debye's law Cᵥ ∝ T³. In metals, electrons add a linear γT term. The Debye temperature θD marks the crossover where all phonon modes wake up.

🔥 Phase II — The Umklapp Awakening

As temperature rises, phonon-phonon scattering turns umklapp — crystal momentum is no longer conserved, and thermal conductivity peaks around θD/5 before collapsing as T⁻¹ at high temperatures. The Wiedemann–Franz law links this chaos to electrical resistance.

📏 Phase III — Thermal Expansion

Heat doesn't just move; it grows the lattice. Because the interatomic potential is anharmonic (asymmetric), warming shifts the average bond length. At T = 0 K, even quantum zero-point motion leaves a residual expansion — the Grüneisen parameter γG quantifies how much the lattice dances.

⚡ Phase IV — Thermoelectric Frontier

A temperature gradient across a metal drives electrons uphill — the Seebeck effect. Reverse the current and you pump heat — the Peltier effect. These phenomena are the backbone of thermoelectric generators and solid-state cooling.

📚 Thermal Physics — Overview

Thermal properties of solids are governed by two quantum fluids: phonons (quantized lattice vibrations) and electrons near the Fermi surface. Their excitations store heat, transport it, and couple to the lattice geometry through anharmonicity.

1. Debye Model — Lattice Heat Capacity

The Debye model treats the crystal as a continuous elastic medium up to a cutoff ωD. Defining x = ℏω/kBT and xD = θD/T, the heat capacity is

$$C_V = 9R \left(\frac{T}{\theta_D}\right)^3 \int_0^{x_D} \frac{x^4 e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2} \, dx$$

At low T this gives CV ∝ T³; at high T it approaches the Dulong–Petit limit 3R.

2. Einstein Model — Single-Frequency Approximation

Einstein assumes all 3N oscillators vibrate at one frequency ωE. The heat capacity is

$$C_V = 3R \left(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2 \frac{e^{\theta_E/T}}{(e^{\theta_E/T} - 1)^2}$$

It also tends to 3R at high T, but falls off exponentially at low T because it misses low-frequency acoustic modes.

3. Electronic Heat Capacity — Free-Electron Gas

Only electrons within ~ kBT of the Fermi surface can be thermally excited. This gives a linear term

$$C_{V,\text{el}} = \gamma T, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\pi^2}{3} k_B^2 g(E_F)$$

For copper, γ ≈ 0.695 mJ/mol·K².

4. Thermal Conductivity — Boltzmann Transport

From kinetic theory, κ = (1/3) Cv v ℓ. Phonon mean free path is limited by boundaries, impurities, and Umklapp scattering. The Matthiessen rule is

$$\frac{1}{\ell} = \frac{1}{\ell_\text{boundary}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\text{impurity}} + \frac{1}{\ell_\text{Umklapp}}$$

At low T, κ ∝ T³; near θD/5 it peaks; at high T, Umklapp limits give κ ∝ 1/T.

5. Thermal Expansion — Grüneisen Theory

The expansion coefficient is linked to the Grüneisen parameter by

$$\alpha = \frac{\gamma_G C_V}{3 B V}, \qquad \gamma_G = -\frac{d(\ln \omega)}{d(\ln V)}$$

In a harmonic crystal γG = 0 and α = 0. Anharmonicity creates γG > 0 and thus expansion.

6. Wiedemann–Franz Law

For metals, the ratio κ/(σT) is the Lorenz number

$$\frac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = L_0 = \frac{\pi^2}{3} \left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2 = 2.44 \times 10^{-8} \, \text{W}\cdot\Omega/\text{K}^2$$

This works because both heat and charge are carried by the same electrons.

7. Thermoelectric Effects

A temperature gradient creates a voltage (Seebeck) and a current pumps heat (Peltier). Their coefficients satisfy Π = ST, and the figure of merit is

$$ZT = \frac{S^2 \sigma T}{\kappa}$$

State-of-the-art materials such as Bi₂Te₃ and SnSe reach ZT ≈ 2–3 at room temperature.

✨ Why does this matter?

Heat capacity is the thermometer of the quantum world. The T³ slope at low temperatures measures the Debye temperature θD — the characteristic energy scale of lattice vibrations. The linear γT intercept reveals the electron density of states at the Fermi level. Together, these two numbers fingerprint any solid material.

Debye: Cᵥ,ph = 9R (T/θD)³ ∫₀^{θD/T} [x⁴ eˣ / (eˣ−1)²] dx

κ = κ_ph + κ_el. Umklapp peak ~50 K. Electron κ_el ∝ T⁻¹ at low T.

✨ Why does this matter?

The peak in κ(T) is a fingerprint of phonon-phonon scattering. Diamond has exceptionally high thermal conductivity because its rigid tetrahedral bonds suppress anharmonicity. In metals, the Wiedemann–Franz law lets you predict κ from electrical resistivity — a cornerstone of materials science.

$$\kappa_{\text{ph}} = \frac{1}{3}\, C_V\, v_s\, \ell_{\text{ph}}$$ $$\text{Wiedemann–Franz:} \quad \frac{\kappa_{\text{el}}}{\sigma T} = L_0 = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2 = 2.44 \times 10^{-8}\;\text{W}\cdot\Omega\cdot\text{K}^{-2}$$
