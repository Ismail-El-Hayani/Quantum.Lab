📖 The Story — From Sand to Silicon: The Rise of Semiconductors

In 1833, Michael Faraday noticed something peculiar: silver sulfide's conductivity increased with temperature — the opposite of metals. For a century this remained a curiosity. Then came the 1947 discovery of the transistor at Bell Labs (Bardeen, Brattain, Shockley), and the semiconductor revolution began. The key insight: in an intrinsic (undoped) semiconductor, every electron excited to the conduction band leaves behind a hole in the valence band — a quasiparticle that behaves like a positively charged carrier. The product np = ni² is fixed at a given temperature, determined by the bandgap and the effective masses.

🧩 Intrinsic vs Extrinsic

An intrinsic semiconductor has no impurities — electrons and holes are created in pairs by thermal excitation. The Fermi level sits near mid-gap. Adding donor atoms (P, As) or acceptors (B, Al) dopes the material n-type or p-type, shifting the Fermi level and creating a majority carrier. In the Playground you can toggle doping and watch the Fermi level move.

🔬 Bandgap: The Gatekeeper

The bandgap Eg determines everything. Si (1.12 eV, indirect) needs more thermal energy to excite carriers than Ge (0.67 eV). GaAs (1.42 eV, direct) emits light efficiently — hence LEDs and lasers. The Varshni model captures how Eg shrinks with temperature as lattice vibrations blur the periodic potential: Eg(T) = Eg0 − αT²/(T+β).

🎓 Theory — Carrier Statistics, Band Structure, and Transport

In an intrinsic semiconductor the carrier concentrations are governed by Fermi-Dirac statistics. The density of electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band are:

$$n = \int_{E_c}^{\infty} D_c(E)\,f(E)\,dE,\qquad p = \int_{-\infty}^{E_v} D_v(E)\,[1 - f(E)]\,dE$$

where \(D_c(E)\) and \(D_v(E)\) are the density of states in each band, and \(f(E) = 1/[1 + e^{(E-E_F)/k_BT}]\) is the Fermi-Dirac distribution. For non-degenerate semiconductors (\(E_F\) more than \(3k_BT\) from either band edge), the Maxwell-Boltzmann approximation holds and we get closed forms:

$$n = N_c\,e^{-(E_c - E_F)/k_BT}, \qquad p = N_v\,e^{-(E_F - E_v)/k_BT}$$

The effective densities of states are \(N_c = 2(2\pi m_e^* k_BT / h^2)^{3/2}\) and \(N_v = 2(2\pi m_h^* k_BT / h^2)^{3/2}\).

1. Intrinsic Carrier Concentration

Multiplying the expressions for n and p gives the mass-action law:

$$n_i^2 = np = N_c N_v\,e^{-E_g/k_BT}, \qquad n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\,e^{-E_g/2k_BT}$$

The intrinsic Fermi level sits near mid-gap: \(E_i = \frac{E_c + E_v}{2} + \frac{k_BT}{2}\ln\frac{N_v}{N_c}\). In the intrinsic case, n = p = ni.

2. Doping and the Fermi Level

With donor concentration \(N_D\) and acceptor concentration \(N_A\), charge neutrality requires \(n + N_A^- = p + N_D^+\). For non-degenerate doping at moderate temperatures:

$$n \approx N_D - N_A \;\;(\text{n-type}), \qquad p \approx N_A - N_D \;\;(\text{p-type})$$

The Fermi level shifts accordingly: \(E_F = E_i + k_BT\ln(n/n_i)\) for n-type, \(E_F = E_i - k_BT\ln(p/n_i)\) for p-type.

3. Temperature Regimes

Freeze-out (low T): carriers are bound to dopant atoms; conductivity is minimal.
Extrinsic (mid T): dopants are fully ionised, carrier concentration is constant; mobility peaks then falls via phonon scattering.
Intrinsic (high T): thermal generation dominates and ni² = np; conductivity rises exponentially.

4. Conductivity and Mobility

The total conductivity combines electron and hole contributions:

$$\sigma = e(n\mu_e + p\mu_h), \qquad \mu_e = \frac{e\tau_e}{m_e^*}, \qquad \mu_h = \frac{e\tau_h}{m_h^*}$$

Mobility degrades with temperature via phonon scattering (\(\mu \propto T^{-3/2}\) for acoustic phonons) and with doping via ionised impurity scattering (\(\mu \propto T^{3/2}/N_i\)).

5. Electric Field and Band Bending

An applied electric field tilts the band edges linearly with position: Ec(x) = Ec0 − eEx. This drives electrons downhill in the conduction band and holes uphill in the valence band. The drift velocities are:

$$v_{d,e} = -\mu_e E, \qquad v_{d,h} = \mu_h E$$

💡 Physics Insight: Temperature, Doping, and Fields

Temperature controls thermal carrier generation via the Varshni bandgap and the Boltzmann factor exp(−Eg/2kT).
Doping shifts the Fermi level: donors (ND) pull EF toward Ec; acceptors (NA) push it toward Ev.
Electric fields tilt the bands, causing electrons to drift in the conduction band and holes in the valence band.

$$E_g(T) = E_{g0} - \frac{\alpha T^2}{T + \beta} \quad \text{(Varshni)}$$ $$n_i = \sqrt{N_c N_v}\,e^{-E_g/2k_BT} \qquad E_F = E_i + k_BT\ln\frac{n}{n_i} \qquad \sigma = e(n\mu_e + p\mu_h)$$
