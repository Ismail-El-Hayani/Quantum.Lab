Fermi Surface & Statistics

Electrons near the Fermi surface carry all the heat and current. Explore thermal smearing, k-space occupation, and material-dependent Fermi surfaces.

📖 The Story — The Fermi Surface and Why It Matters

In a metal, electrons fill up energy states from the bottom, two per state (spin up and down), until they reach the Fermi energy EF. The boundary in momentum space between filled and empty states is the Fermi surface — a sharp sphere at absolute zero, blurred by temperature into a thermal shell of width ~kBT. This surface is everything: only electrons near it can respond to electric fields, carry heat, or absorb light. The shape of the Fermi surface determines whether a material is a good metal, a superconductor, or a strange metal with non-Fermi-liquid behaviour.

🧩 The Pauli Principle in Action

The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons from occupying the same quantum state. This forces electrons to stack up in energy until the last one sits at EF. In copper, EF ≈ 7 eV, corresponding to a Fermi temperature of 81,000 K — far above room temperature. This is why metals feel "cold" to the touch: most electrons are frozen in place by Pauli blocking and cannot absorb thermal energy.

🔬 Why Only k_BT Matters

At room temperature, kBT ≈ 25 meV — a tiny fraction of EF. Only electrons within this energy window of the Fermi surface can be thermally excited. This explains why the electronic specific heat is Cv = γT (linear in T) instead of the classical 3NkB: 99% of electrons are Pauli-blocked and contribute nothing. 🎓 Theory — Fermi-Dirac Statistics and the Fermi Surface

Electrons are fermions: they obey the Pauli exclusion principle and fill energy states according to the Fermi-Dirac distribution:

$$f(E) = \frac{1}{e^{(E - \mu)/(k_B T)} + 1}$$

where \(\mu\) is the chemical potential (equal to \(E_F\) at \(T = 0\)) and \(k_B T\) is the thermal energy. At \(T = 0\), \(f(E)\) is a perfect step: 1 for \(E < E_F\), 0 for \(E > E_F\). At finite \(T\), the step is smoothed over an energy window \(\sim k_B T\).

1. Fermi Wavevector and Energy

For a free-electron gas in 3D, the Fermi wavevector and energy are:

$$k_F = (3\pi^2 n)^{1/3}, \qquad E_F = \frac{\hbar^2 k_F^2}{2m}$$

where \(n\) is the electron density. The Fermi temperature \(T_F = E_F / k_B\) is typically 10⁴–10⁵ K for metals, meaning room temperature is deeply degenerate.

2. Chemical Potential at Finite Temperature

The Sommerfeld expansion gives the temperature-dependent chemical potential in 3D:

$$\mu(T) \approx E_F \left[1 - \frac{\pi^2}{12}\left(\frac{k_B T}{E_F}\right)^2\right]$$

valid when \(T \ll T_F\). The correction is tiny at room temperature (\(\sim 0.01\%\)) but becomes significant near \(T_F\).

3. Electronic Specific Heat

Only electrons within \(k_B T\) of \(E_F\) can absorb thermal energy. This gives a linear-in-\(T\) specific heat instead of the classical Dulong-Petit value:

$$C_v = \gamma T, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\pi^2}{3} k_B^2 g(E_F) = \frac{\pi^2}{2} \frac{N k_B^2}{E_F}$$

The coefficient \(\gamma\) is proportional to the density of states at \(E_F\). Heavy-fermion materials have enormous \(g(E_F)\) and thus enormous \(\gamma\) — up to 1000× larger than copper.

4. Wiedemann-Franz Law

Because both charge and heat are carried by the same quasiparticles near \(E_F\), the ratio of thermal to electrical conductivity is universal:

$$\frac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = L = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2 \approx 2.44 \times 10^{-8}\;\text{W}\Omega/\text{K}^2$$

This holds for Fermi liquids (metals) but breaks down in semiconductors (phonons carry heat) and in non-Fermi-liquids (strange metals, superconductors).

5. Degeneracy Pressure

The Pauli principle creates a pressure even at \(T = 0\): electrons cannot all collapse into the lowest state. The degeneracy pressure for non-relativistic fermions is:

$$P \propto n^{5/3}, \qquad P_{\text{deg}} = \frac{2}{5} n E_F$$

This pressure supports white dwarfs against gravitational collapse and prevents metals from imploding under their own electronic charge. The exponent 5/3 reflects the 3D density of states.

📈 Fermi-Dirac Statistics

The distribution f(E) controls which states are occupied. The derivative −∂f/∂E is a thermal broadening kernel that picks out the active states near EF.

Fermi-Dirac f(E)

Occupation probability: f(E) = [1 + exp((E − EF)/(kBT))]−1.

At T = 0, f(E) is a perfect step: every state below EF is filled, every state above is empty. At finite T the edge is smoothed over an energy window ~kBT. Yellow dashed = T = 0 step; dotted magenta = Maxwell-Boltzmann classical limit.

Derivative ∂f/∂E

Thermal broadening kernel. Width ≈ 3.5 kBT around EF.

−∂f/∂E is a thermal-broadened delta function centred on μ. Its total area is conserved (equals 1), but its width grows with T because more states become thermally accessible. Only electrons inside this window respond to external perturbations.

Occupation Number n(E)

Electron occupancy near EF. Smears out as temperature rises.

⚙ Applications & Extensions

Real consequences of Fermi-Dirac statistics in materials.

🧲 Pauli Exclusion Principle

Pauli Paramagnetic Susceptibility

No two electrons share all four quantum numbers.

Pauli paramagnetic susceptibility χP(T) is proportional to the density of states at the Fermi level, χP ∼ μB² μ(EF). It is nearly constant at low T and drops once kBT becomes comparable to EF.

🔥 Thermionic Emission

Thermionic Emission J(T)

Richardson-Dushman: J = A T² exp(−φ/(kBT)).

Only electrons in the high-energy tail of the Fermi-Dirac distribution escape the work-function barrier φ. Materials with larger φ need higher temperatures for significant emission.

⭐ White-Dwarf Degeneracy Pressure

Work Function Distribution

Energy to lift an electron from EF to vacuum.

White-dwarf degeneracy pressure P ∝ n5/3 for non-relativistic fermions: the same Pauli pressure that keeps the star from collapsing prevents metals from collapsing under their own electronic charge. The exponent 5/3 arises because the Fermi energy grows with density while the pressure is the energy density.

💡 Physical Insights

The Fermi-Dirac distribution f(E) = [exp((E−μ)/kBT) + 1]−1 is the cornerstone of electron behaviour in solids. At absolute zero it is a perfect step function: all states below EF are filled, all above are empty. This Pauli blocking is why metals have Pauli paramagnetism, a specific heat proportional to T, and thermal conductivities carried almost entirely by electrons.

The finite-temperature correction to the chemical potential in 3D is μ(T) ≈ EF [1 − (π2/12)(kBT/EF)2], valid when T ≪ TF = EF/kB. Even at room temperature, typical metals have T/TF ~ 10−2, so only a tiny fraction of electrons within kBT of EF can participate in transport or thermal excitation. This explains why the electronic specific heat Cv = γT is so small compared with the classical value 3NkB.

The Wiedemann-Franz law κ/(σT) = L = (π2/3)(kB/e)2 = 2.44×10−8 WΩ/K2 holds because both charge and heat are carried by the same quasiparticles near EF. A Lorenz number close to this universal value is a fingerprint of Fermi-liquid behaviour. In semiconductors phonons carry heat while electrons carry charge, so L is violated.
