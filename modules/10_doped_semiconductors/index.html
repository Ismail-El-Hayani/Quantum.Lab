Doped Semiconductors

Control conductivity with impurities. Shift the Fermi level by donor and acceptor doping. Master extrinsic semiconductors.

📖 The Story — Engineering Conductivity with Impurities

In 1947, the transistor at Bell Labs used a doped germanium crystal — a pure semiconductor intentionally contaminated with trace amounts of specific impurities. This was the breakthrough: by adding donor atoms (Group V: P, As, Sb) or acceptor atoms (Group III: B, Al, Ga), you can tune the carrier type and concentration independently of temperature. Modern CMOS fabrication uses ion implantation — firing dopant ions at a silicon wafer — to create nanoscale n-type and p-type regions that form transistors, the building blocks of every microchip.

🧩 Donors vs Acceptors

Donors (P, As) have five valence electrons; four bond with Si, the fifth is loosely bound (~45 meV) and easily ionised into the conduction band, leaving a fixed positive charge. Acceptors (B, Al) have three valence electrons; they create a "missing bond" that captures an electron from the valence band, producing a mobile hole and a fixed negative charge.

🔬 Compensation

When both donors and acceptors are present, they compensate each other. The net carrier density is |Nd − Na| in the extrinsic regime. Heavily compensated material behaves like a lightly doped semiconductor — with much lower mobility due to charged impurity scattering. This is a critical issue in radiation-damaged detectors and poorly annealed implants.

🎓 Theory — Charge Neutrality, Fermi Level, and Scattering

In a doped semiconductor, thermal equilibrium demands charge neutrality: the net charge from mobile carriers and ionised impurities must sum to zero:

$$n + N_a^- = p + N_d^+$$

For shallow donors/acceptors at room temperature, \(N_d^+ \approx N_d\) and \(N_a^- \approx N_a\). The solution for the majority carrier is:

$$n = \frac{N_d - N_a}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\frac{N_d - N_a}{2}\right)^2 + n_i^2}, \qquad p = \frac{n_i^2}{n}$$

When \(|N_d - N_a| \gg n_i\) the material is extrinsic; when \(|N_d - N_a| \ll n_i\) it becomes intrinsic.

1. Fermi Level Shift

The intrinsic Fermi level sits near mid-gap. Doping moves it:

$$E_F = E_i + k_BT\ln\frac{n}{n_i} \quad (\text{n-type}), \qquad E_F = E_i - k_BT\ln\frac{p}{n_i} \quad (\text{p-type})$$

The shift grows logarithmically with doping: \(\Delta E_F \propto k_BT \ln(N_d/n_i)\). Heavy doping pushes \(E_F\) into the band — the degenerate regime where Maxwell-Boltzmann statistics break down.

2. Three Temperature Regimes

Freeze-out (T → 0): carriers freeze onto dopant sites; n and p vanish.
Extrinsic (50–500 K): dopants fully ionised; n ≈ Nd − Na (flat plateau).
Intrinsic (high T): thermal generation overwhelms doping; n ≈ p ≈ ni.

3. Mobility and Conductivity

Total conductivity combines both carriers:

$$\sigma = e(n\mu_n + p\mu_p), \qquad \mu_n \propto T^{-3/2}, \qquad \mu_p \propto T^{-3/2}$$

At low T, ionised-impurity scattering (\(\mu \propto T^{3/2}/N_i)\) dominates. At high T, phonon scattering (\(\mu \propto T^{-3/2}\)) dominates. The peak in \(\sigma(T)\) arises from the transition between these regimes.

4. Compensation

When \(N_d \approx N_a\) the material is compensated. Although the net carrier density \(|N_d - N_a|\) is small, the total ionised impurity density \(N_d + N_a\) is large, causing strong scattering and low mobility. This is the bane of radiation-damaged detectors and poorly annealed ion implants.

📈 Fermi Level Shift

N-doping pushes EF toward the conduction-band minimum; P-doping pulls it toward the valence-band maximum.

What it shows: How donor/acceptor doping pushes the Fermi level toward the conduction or valence band edge.
Why it matters: The Fermi-level position decides whether electrons or holes dominate — the core principle behind every transistor and diode.

📈 Carrier Density vs Temperature

Freeze-out → extrinsic plateau → intrinsic. Semiconductor devices are engineered to operate in the plateau.

What it shows: The three temperature regimes — freeze-out, extrinsic, and intrinsic — where carrier control shifts from dopants to thermal generation.
Why it matters: Devices are engineered to operate in the flat extrinsic plateau so doping alone sets conductivity.

📈 Doped Conductivity σ(T)

Ionized-impurity scattering at low T, phonon scattering at high T. Conductivity reflects both carrier density and mobility.

What it shows: How conductivity scales with doping density and temperature, reflecting the balance between carrier density and mobility.
Why it matters: Engineers choose doping levels precisely to hit target sheet resistance for interconnects and channel layers.
