GaAs
λ = 870 nm · Eg = 1.42 eV · Direct gap
Gallium arsenide is the classic III–V direct-gap laser material. Used in CD/DVD drives, optical mice, and short-range telecom its cleaved crystal facets provide the natural cavity mirrors (R≈0.30) without external coating.
InGaAsP
λ = 1550 nm · Eg = 0.80 eV · Quaternary alloy
Indium gallium arsenide phosphide dominates long-haul fiber optics. By tuning the In/Ga and As/P ratios the band gap is dialled to match either the 1.3 µm (O-band) or 1.55 µm (C-band) telecom windows with minimal dispersion or attenuation as shown in the lecture fiber-absorption curve.
GaN
λ = 405 nm · Eg = 3.40 eV · Direct gap · Wurtzite
Gallium nitride enabled the solid-state lighting revolution. Its wide direct gap produces violet/blue light used in Blu-ray disc lasers (405 nm) and, when combined with a yellow phosphor, white LEDs. Typically grown on sapphire or SiC substrates; lattice matching is a major engineering challenge.
GaInNAs(Sb)
λ ≈ 1.3 µm · Dilute-nitride quantum well · Direct gap
A dilute-nitride quantum-well active layer inside a VCSEL emitting at 1.3 µm. Used in 10 GHz telecom devices and as an optical amplifier from 1.2–1.6 µm. Adding small amounts of nitrogen to GaInAs strongly reduces the band gap while keeping the lattice parameter close to GaAs — a key trick for fabricating long-wavelength devices on GaAs substrates.

✨ How does a laser actually work?

A laser is an optical amplifier inside a resonator. The lecture distinguishes between three-step and four-level lasers; here we show the three-step idea in action. The animation displays the three essential ingredients: (1) Pumping injects energy to excite electrons from level N₁ to higher levels (N₂, N₃). In a four-level scheme the lower laser level is quickly depopulated, making inversion easier. The slider controls pumping strength; below threshold electrons relax spontaneously, while above threshold population inversion (N₂>N₁) enables a cascade. (2) Stimulated emission (page 2): a seed photon with hν = E₂−E₁ passing through the inverted medium triggers an excited electron to drop down. The emitted photon is identical in phase, direction, and polarization to the seed — unlike spontaneous emission which has a wide angular distribution and broad spectrum. Cyan photons in the animation represent this coherent clone. (3) The cavity recycles photons on each round trip. Mirrors are either cleaved crystal facets (reflectivity R≈0.30 in III–V semiconductors) or dielectric Bragg reflectors (R>0.99 in VCSELs). One mirror is intentionally leaky — escaping photons form the output beam.

Direct vs indirect gap (page 8) is crucial for semiconductor lasers. In direct-gap materials (GaAs, GaN, InP, InGaAsP), an electron losing energy creates a photon directly. In indirect-gap materials (Si, Ge), the transition requires a k-vector change and emits a phonon instead — almost no light. This is why lasers use III–V or III–N compounds and never bulk silicon. Homojunction vs heterojunction (page 13): early diode lasers were homojunctions (equal band gap, different doping) — they had to be cooled because electrons and holes spread out and recombined non-radiatively. Modern devices use double heterojunctions: wide-gap cladding layers (e.g. AlGaAs around GaAs) confine both the carriers and the optical mode inside the thin active region, acting as a waveguide. Quantum wells (page 14): if the active layer is extremely thin (nanometers), the electrons are confined in one dimension, splitting the bands into discrete sub-bands — this dramatically changes the density of states and produces sharper emission linewidths than bulk lasers. Telecom windows (page 9): fiber-optic glass has minimum absorption near 1.3 µm (zero dispersion) and 1.55 µm (minimum attenuation). That is why InGaAsP lasers are tuned precisely to those wavelengths. Brewster angle (page 6): gas lasers like HeNe use external Brewster windows to polarize the intracavity light. Photons polarized in the plane of incidence pass with zero reflection loss; the orthogonal polarization is rejected, producing a strongly polarized output.
