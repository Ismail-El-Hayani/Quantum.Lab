Laser Physics

Build a laser cavity, pump electrons, and watch stimulated emission create a coherent beam. Interact with real photon dynamics at the micrometer scale.

🔦 What is a laser?

LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. The first working laser was demonstrated by Theodore Maiman in May 1960 at Hughes Research Laboratories — a flashlamp-pumped ruby rod with silver-coated ends acting as cavity mirrors. Unlike ordinary light sources (light bulbs, LEDs) that emit incoherently in all directions, a laser produces light that is coherent (fixed phase relation), monochromatic (narrow spectral linewidth), and directional (low divergence beam).

⚙ Essential ingredients

Every laser has four basic components:

  • Gain medium — a material that can amplify light via stimulated emission. This can be a crystal (Nd:YAG), gas (HeNe, CO₂), semiconductor (GaAs), or doped fibre (Er fibre).
  • Pumping source — energy input that excites the gain medium. Electrical current (laser diodes), flashlamps (solid-state lasers), gas discharge (HeNe, CO₂), or another laser.
  • Optical resonator (cavity) — two mirrors that provide feedback. One mirror is highly reflective, the other partially transmitting (the output coupler). The simplest is the Fabry–Pérot cavity.
  • Population inversion — a non-equilibrium condition where more atoms/molecules/electrons occupy the upper laser level than the lower level (N₂ > N₁). This is a prerequisite for net optical gain.

When a seed photon travelling at the right frequency encounters an excited atom, it triggers stimulated emission: the emitted photon is identical in phase, polarisation, and direction to the incident photon — the essence of coherent amplification.

🧬 Laser types & their applications

Gas lasers
HeNe (633 nm, red): alignment, holography, barcode scanners.
CO₂ (10.6 µm, IR): industrial cutting, welding, engraving, surgery.
Ar⁺ (488/514 nm): flow cytometry, retinal photocoagulation.
Solid-state lasers
Nd:YAG (1064 nm): drilling, welding, range-finding, Q-switched for tattoo removal.
Ti:sapphire (tunable 650–1100 nm): femtosecond pulses, spectroscopy.
Ruby (694 nm, pulsed): the original laser.
Semiconductor lasers
Laser diodes: telecom (1310/1550 nm), CD/DVD/Blu-ray (780/650/405 nm), LIDAR, optical mice, fibre-pump modules.
VCSELs: surface-emitting, used in 3D sensing (iPhone Face ID) and data-centre interconnects.
Other types
Fibre lasers (Yb, Er, Tm): high-power industrial cutting, telecom amplifiers.
Dye lasers: broadly tunable visible light, used in spectroscopy.
Quantum cascade lasers: mid-IR to THz, trace-gas sensing, free-space coms.

📜 Laser physics in context

The theoretical foundation was laid by Albert Einstein in 1917, who derived the A and B coefficients for spontaneous and stimulated emission from thermodynamic arguments — seven years before the quantum-mechanical description of light. In 1954 Charles Townes built the first maser (microwave amplification). Schawlow and Townes (1958) extended the concept to optical frequencies. Maiman's ruby laser followed in 1960.

The semiconductor laser was demonstrated by four groups independently in 1962 (Hall, Nathan, Quist, Holonyak). The double heterojunction (Alferov, Kroemer, Nobel 2000) enabled continuous-wave room-temperature operation by confining both carriers and the optical mode. Quantum wells (1970s) further improved efficiency by discretising the density of states. Today's VCSELs and quantum cascade lasers represent the state of the art in semiconductor photonics.

⚠ Laser safety

Lasers are classified by their potential to cause harm:

  • Class 1 — safe under all conditions (e.g. CD/DVD players, enclosed fibre systems).
  • Class 2 — low-power visible (< 1 mW); blink reflex protects the eye (e.g. laser pointers).
  • Class 3R — up to 5 mW; direct intra-beam viewing is hazardous.
  • Class 3B — up to 500 mW; eye hazard even for diffuse reflections.
  • Class 4 — > 500 mW; skin and fire hazard; requires interlock, beam block, and eyewear.

Invisible IR lasers (CO₂ at 10.6 µm, telecom at 1550 nm) are particularly dangerous because the blink reflex does not respond to IR light. Always wear appropriate OD-rated eyewear for the wavelength in use.

📐 Einstein coefficients

Einstein (1917) described the interaction between light and a two-level system using three coefficients:

  • A₂₁ — spontaneous emission rate (s⁻¹). An excited atom decays without external stimulus, emitting a photon in a random direction and phase. Lifetime τ₂ = 1/A₂₁.
  • B₁₂ — absorption rate coefficient. An incident photon at resonance is absorbed, exciting the atom from level 1 to level 2. Transition rate = B₁₂ ρ(ν).
  • B₂₁ — stimulated emission rate coefficient. An incident photon triggers the excited atom to emit a copy photon. Transition rate = B₂₁ ρ(ν).
$$g_1 B_{12} = g_2 B_{21} \qquad A_{21} = \frac{8\pi h \nu^3}{c^3} B_{21}$$

where g₁, g₂ are the degeneracies of levels 1 and 2. The second relation shows that spontaneous emission dominates at high frequencies (UV, X-ray) while stimulated emission dominates at low frequencies (radio, microwave). This is why masers (microwaves) were built before lasers (visible light).

📈 Rate equations

For a 4-level laser system (the most common architecture), the dynamics are described by coupled differential equations:

$$\frac{dN_2}{dt} = R_p - \frac{N_2}{\tau_2} - (N_2 - N_1)\sigma v N_\gamma$$ $$\frac{dN_1}{dt} = \frac{N_2}{\tau_{21}} - \frac{N_1}{\tau_{10}} + (N_2 - N_1)\sigma v N_\gamma$$ $$\frac{dN_\gamma}{dt} = (N_2 - N_1)\sigma v N_\gamma - \frac{N_\gamma}{\tau_\text{cav}} + \beta\frac{N_2}{\tau_2}$$

Rₚ = pumping rate | τ₂ = upper-level lifetime | τ₂₁ = laser transition lifetime | τ₁₀ = lower-level relaxation lifetime | σ = stimulated emission cross-section | v = speed of light in medium | Nγ = photon density | τcav = cavity photon lifetime | β = spontaneous emission factor (fraction of spontaneous photons that couple into the laser mode). The βN₂/τ₂ term seeds the laser from spontaneous emission and is crucial for start-up.

🎯 Threshold condition

Lasing begins when the round-trip gain equals the round-trip loss. This defines the threshold gain:

$$g_{\text{th}} = \alpha + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\frac{1}{R_1 R_2}$$

α = distributed loss coefficient (cm⁻¹) due to absorption and scattering in the cavity. The logarithmic term represents the mirror loss: for identical mirrors with R = 0.30 (cleaved facet), this contributes ≈ 120 cm⁻¹ for a 2000 nm cavity. The population inversion at threshold is:

$$\Delta N_{\text{th}} = N_2 - N_1 = \frac{g_{\text{th}}}{\sigma}$$

Below threshold the laser acts as an LED (broad spectrum, low power); above threshold the output rises steeply and the linewidth narrows dramatically. The L–L curve (light vs pump power) shows a clear "knee" at threshold — a hallmark of laser operation.

🔄 Fabry–Pérot cavity modes

The optical resonator supports only discrete longitudinal modes satisfying the standing-wave condition:

$$\lambda_m = \frac{2nL}{m} \qquad \nu_m = \frac{mc}{2nL} \qquad \Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}} = \frac{c}{2nL}$$

where m is an integer (mode index), n is the refractive index, L is the cavity length, and c is the speed of light. ΔνFSR is the free spectral range — the frequency spacing between adjacent modes.

The spectral width of each cavity resonance depends on the mirror reflectivity via the finesse:

$$\mathcal{F} = \frac{\pi\sqrt{R}}{1-R} \qquad \delta\nu = \frac{\Delta\nu_{\text{FSR}}}{\mathcal{F}}$$

A higher finesse means narrower resonances and lower threshold. For R = 0.99, ℱ ≈ 300; for R = 0.30, ℱ ≈ 2. Only modes that fall within the gain bandwidth of the laser medium can oscillate. In semiconductor lasers the gain bandwidth is broad (tens of nm) so multiple longitudinal modes can lase simultaneously unless a wavelength-selective element (grating, etalon) is introduced.

🧪 Semiconductor laser specifics

Semiconductor lasers differ fundamentally from gas and solid-state lasers because the gain medium is a forward-biased p–n junction.

  • Direct vs indirect band gap: In direct-gap materials (GaAs, InP, GaN) the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same k-vector. Electron–hole recombination directly produces a photon. In indirect-gap materials (Si, Ge) a phonon must participate — the radiative efficiency is negligible.
  • Double heterojunction: A thin lower-bandgap active layer (e.g. GaAs) is sandwiched between wider-bandgap cladding layers (e.g. AlGaAs). This confines both electrons/holes (potential well) and the optical mode (refractive-index step). The result: drastically lower threshold currents and room-temperature CW operation.
  • Quantum wells: When the active layer is thinner than the de Broglie wavelength (~10 nm), the density of states becomes step-like (2D) rather than parabolic (3D). This concentrates gain at specific wavelengths, improves temperature stability, and lowers threshold further.
  • Gain spectrum: Semiconductor gain is broad (20–50 nm) due to the Fermi–Dirac distribution of carriers. The peak shifts with carrier density (band filling) and temperature (Varshni shift).
$$\text{Direct gap: } \lambda(\text{nm}) = \frac{1240}{E_g(\text{eV})} \qquad \text{Gain: } g(\hbar\omega) = g_0 \left[f_c(\hbar\omega) - f_v(\hbar\omega)\right]$$

🧪 Common Laser Materials

GaAs
λ = 870 nm · Eg = 1.42 eV · Direct gap
Gallium arsenide is the classic III–V direct-gap laser material. Used in CD/DVD drives, optical mice, and short-range telecom; its cleaved crystal facets provide natural cavity mirrors (R≈0.30) without external coating.
InGaAsP
λ = 1550 nm · Eg = 0.80 eV · Quaternary alloy
Indium gallium arsenide phosphide dominates long-haul fiber optics. By tuning the In/Ga and As/P ratios the band gap matches either the 1.3 µm (O-band) or 1.55 µm (C-band) telecom windows with minimal dispersion or attenuation.
GaN
λ = 405 nm · Eg = 3.40 eV · Direct gap · Wurtzite
Gallium nitride enabled the solid-state lighting revolution. Its wide direct gap produces violet/blue light used in Blu-ray disc lasers (405 nm) and, combined with a yellow phosphor, white LEDs. Typically grown on sapphire or SiC substrates.
GaInNAs(Sb)
λ ≈ 1.3 µm · Dilute-nitride quantum well · Direct gap
A dilute-nitride quantum-well active layer inside a VCSEL emitting at 1.3 µm. Adding small amounts of nitrogen to GaInAs strongly reduces the band gap while keeping the lattice parameter close to GaAs — a key trick for fabricating long-wavelength devices on GaAs substrates.

✨ How does a laser actually work?

A laser is an optical amplifier inside a resonator. The animation displays the three essential ingredients: (1) Pumping injects energy to excite electrons from level N₁ to higher levels. Below threshold electrons relax spontaneously; above threshold population inversion (N₂>N₁) enables a cascade. (2) Stimulated emission: a seed photon with hν = E₂−E₁ passing through the inverted medium triggers an excited electron to drop down. The emitted photon is identical in phase, direction, and polarization to the seed — unlike spontaneous emission. (3) The cavity recycles photons on each round trip. Mirrors are either cleaved crystal facets (R≈0.30) or dielectric Bragg reflectors (R>0.99). One mirror is intentionally leaky — escaping photons form the output beam.

Direct vs indirect gap is crucial for semiconductor lasers. In direct-gap materials (GaAs, GaN, InP, InGaAsP) an electron losing energy creates a photon directly. In indirect-gap materials (Si, Ge) the transition requires a k-vector change and emits a phonon instead — almost no light. Homojunction vs heterojunction: modern devices use double heterojunctions with wide-gap cladding layers that confine both carriers and the optical mode inside the thin active region. Quantum wells split bands into discrete sub-bands, producing sharper emission linewidths than bulk lasers. Telecom windows: glass has minimum absorption near 1.3 µm (zero dispersion) and 1.55 µm (minimum attenuation), so InGaAsP lasers are tuned precisely to those wavelengths.

$$N_2 > N_1 \;\;\text{(inversion)} \qquad \frac{dN_{\gamma}}{dt} = R_{\text{stim}} - \frac{N_{\gamma}}{\tau_{\text{cav}}} \qquad g_{\text{th}} = \alpha + \frac{1}{2L}\ln\frac{1}{R^2}$$

📈 Light–Light (L–L) Curve

The L–L curve plots output power against pumping rate. Below threshold the output grows slowly (spontaneous emission, LED-like). Above threshold the knee marks the onset of lasing — output rises steeply as stimulated emission dominates. The threshold pumping factor is where g₀ = gth.

Threshold pth — Slope efficiency — Current pump —
$$P_{\text{out}} \propto \begin{cases} \eta_{\text{sp}} \cdot (p - 1) & p \le p_{\text{th}} \\ \eta_{\text{stim}} \cdot (p - p_{\text{th}}) & p > p_{\text{th}} \end{cases} \qquad p_{\text{th}} = \text{pumping factor at threshold} $$
