Electrical Conductivity

Drift, scatter, conduct. Watch electrons move through a crystal lattice under electric field.

📖 The Story — Why Metals Conduct and Insulators Don't

Apply an electric field to a metal and electrons drift — slowly, against the random thermal motion — producing a current. The Drude model (1900) captured this with one parameter: the relaxation time τ, the average interval between scattering events. From τ comes conductivity σ = ne²τ/m*, resistivity ρ = 1/σ, and the Hall coefficient. Yet the model fails: it predicts Cv = 3nkB (wrong by a factor of 100) and τ that doesn't match measured mean free paths. The fix came with Sommerfeld (1928), who replaced the classical electron gas with Fermi-Dirac statistics — only electrons near EF participate, giving the correct specific heat and Wiedemann-Franz law.

🧩 Drude vs Sommerfeld

Drude treated all electrons as classical particles sharing thermal energy kBT. Sommerfeld showed that Pauli blocking freezes 99% of electrons — only those within kBT of EF can scatter. This fixes the specific heat (from 3nkB to γT) and the Wiedemann-Franz ratio, while leaving σ essentially unchanged because the Fermi velocity vF replaces the thermal velocity.

🔬 Scattering and Matthiessen's Rule

Electrons scatter off phonons (temperature-dependent), impurities (temperature-independent), and boundaries. Matthiessen's rule says these add: 1/τ = 1/τph + 1/τimp + 1/τbd. At low T, impurity scattering dominates and ρ plateaus (residual resistivity). At high T, phonon scattering dominates and ρ ∝ T. The Ioffe-Regel limit kFℓ ≈ 1 marks the breakdown of metallic behaviour.

🎓 Theory — Drude Model, Hall Effect, and Transport

The Drude model treats conduction electrons as a classical gas moving under an electric field E, scattering randomly with average time τ between collisions. The equation of motion gives the drift velocity:

$$v_d = -\frac{eE\tau}{m^*}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m^*}$$

where \(n\) is the carrier density and \(m^*\) the effective mass. The mean free path is \(\ell = v_F \tau\).

1. Hall Effect

In a magnetic field B, the Lorentz force deflects drifting electrons sideways, building up a transverse Hall voltage:

$$R_H = \frac{1}{ne} = \frac{V_H t}{IB}, \qquad \mu = \frac{v_d}{E} = \frac{e\tau}{m^*}$$

The sign of RH reveals the carrier type (electrons vs holes). The mobility μ measures how easily carriers respond to a field.

2. Temperature Dependence of Resistivity

Matthiessen's rule: independent scattering channels add:

$$\frac{1}{\tau} = \frac{1}{\tau_{\text{imp}}} + \frac{1}{\tau_{\text{ph}}(T)}$$

At low T, ρ → ρ0 (impurity residual). At high T, ρ ∝ T (phonon scattering dominates). In superconductors, ρ → 0 below Tc.

3. Wiedemann-Franz Law

In a Fermi liquid, the same electrons carry both charge and heat, giving a universal ratio:

$$\frac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = L = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2 \approx 2.44 \times 10^{-8}\;\text{W}\Omega/\text{K}^2$$

Violations signal non-Fermi-liquid behaviour (e.g. strange metals near a Mott transition) or phonon-dominated heat transport (semiconductors).

4. Ioffe-Regel Criterion

Metallic transport requires the mean free path to exceed the lattice spacing:

$$k_F \ell \gg 1 \;\;\text{(metal)}, \qquad k_F \ell \sim 1 \;\;\text{(bad metal / Anderson localisation)}$$

When kFℓ → 1, the Drude model breaks down and the material enters the "bad metal" regime — the precursor to localisation.

5. Sommerfeld Correction

The Drude model's conductivity is essentially correct because vF replaces the thermal velocity, but it gets the specific heat wrong. Sommerfeld's quantum correction:

$$C_v^{\text{el}} = \gamma T, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{nk_B^2}{E_F}$$

This is ~100× smaller than the classical value 3nkB at room temperature, resolving the major Drude failure.

📈 Conductivity & Transport Curves

Standard solid-state transport relations derived from the Drude model and Bloch-Grüneisen theory.

Drift Velocity vd(E)

Ohmic response: vd = −eEτ/m*. Saturation at high fields.

At low fields the drift velocity grows linearly with E. At very high fields the model saturates because the electron cannot gain more energy between scattering events than it loses at each collision.

Resistivity ρ(T)

Bloch-Grüneisen: ρ ∝ T⁵ (low T) → ρ ∝ T (high T).

At low temperature electron-phonon scattering is frozen out, so ρ is dominated by impurities. As T rises, phonons appear and ρ increases, first as T⁵ and then linearly above the Debye temperature.

I-V Characteristics

Linear (ohmic) vs exponential (diode). Log scale shows crossover.

A resistor follows Ohm's law I = V/R. A p-n diode only conducts once the forward bias exceeds the built-in junction potential; reverse bias gives a tiny leakage current.

Mean Free Path λ(T)

λ = vFτ. Matthiessen rule: 1/λ = 1/λph + 1/λimp.

Matthiessen's rule adds inverse mean free paths because each scattering mechanism shortens the average uninterrupted path. Impurities dominate at low T; phonons dominate at high T.

Hall Voltage VH(B)

VH = IB/(nqd). Sign reveals carrier type: n-type (−) vs p-type (+).

The Hall effect measures carrier density and sign. Electrons bend one way, holes the opposite, so a Hall probe can distinguish n-type and p-type semiconductors without contacts.

Ioffe-Regel Criterion

Metallic limit: kFλ > 1. Below → Anderson localization.

When the mean free path becomes shorter than the Fermi wavelength, the electron waves interfere destructively and the material crosses over from a diffusive metal to an Anderson insulator.

Wiedemann-Franz Ratio

L = κ/(σT) → 2.44×10⁻⁸ WΩ/K² for a Fermi liquid.

The Wiedemann-Franz law states that the ratio of thermal to electrical conductivity divided by temperature is a universal constant. It holds for metals where electrons carry both heat and charge, but fails in semiconductors where phonons dominate heat transport.

💡 Physical Insights

The Drude model treats conduction electrons as a classical gas that accelerates freely between instantaneous scattering events. Its central result is conductivity σ = ne²τ/m*, where n is carrier density, e is the elementary charge, τ is the mean scattering time, and m* is the effective mass. This simple formula captures why metals conduct so well: large n and short m* together give σ ~ 10⁷ S/m.

Scattering limits τ. Phonon scattering grows with temperature while impurity scattering is roughly temperature-independent. In a pure metal at low T, τ can exceed 10⁻¹¹ s and mean free paths can reach micrometres. In dirty alloys or thin films, τ can drop to ~10⁻¹⁵ s and the material approaches the Ioffe-Regel limit where the electron wavefunction localizes.

The Hall sign reveals the carrier type. In metals both electrons and holes contribute; a positive Hall coefficient means hole-like orbits dominate. Semiconductors are deliberately doped to obtain either electron (n-type) or hole (p-type) carriers, and the Hall voltage is the standard way to measure doping density and mobility.
