Superconductivity

Zero resistance. Perfect diamagnetism. Cooper pairs. Explore the quantum state that defies classical intuition.

📖 The Story — When Resistance Vanishes and Fields Are Expelled

In 1911, Heike Kamerlingh Onnes cooled mercury to 4.2 K and watched its electrical resistance drop to exactly zero — the first superconductor. For 46 years the phenomenon defied explanation: a metal with no scattering, no Joule heating, no Ohm's law. Then in 1957, Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer (BCS) showed that below a critical temperature Tc, electrons pair up via phonon exchange into Cooper pairs that condense into a single macroscopic quantum state. A forbidden energy gap 2Δ opens around the Fermi level, blocking the scattering that causes resistance. BCS predicted Δ(0) ≈ 1.76 kBTc — verified to stunning precision.

But superconductivity is more than zero resistance. In 1933, Meissner and Ochsenfeld found that a superconductor actively expels magnetic flux from its interior (B = 0, susceptibility χ = −1) — a thermodynamic state, not a frozen field configuration. The London brothers captured this with a penetration depth λL = √(m/μ₀nse²) over which field decays as B(x) = B0e−x/λL. Type I superconductors lose this state abruptly at a critical field Hc; Type II allow Abrikosov vortices to penetrate between Hc1 and Hc2, enabling the high-field magnets that power MRI scanners and particle accelerators.

🔍 The Discovery

Kamerlingh Onnes liquefied helium in 1908, opening the temperature range below 4 K. Three years later he measured mercury's resistance vanishing at 4.2 K — a phase transition with no classical analogue. The 1957 BCS theory explained it via phonon-mediated pairing and the condensate wavefunction ψ = |ψ|eiφ. The isotope effect (Tc ∝ M−1/2) confirmed the phonon glue. Bednorz and Müller's 1986 cuprate discovery (Tc > 30 K) opened the high-Tc era.

💡 Why It Matters

Superconductors carry current with no loss, enabling MRI magnets (up to 15 T), superconducting quantum computers (transmon qubits), lossless power transmission, and the Josephson effect that defines the SI volt. Type II NbTi and Nb₃Sn magnets drive the LHC and fusion tokamaks. Each application exploits a different facet: zero resistance, perfect diamagnetism, flux quantization, or the macroscopic phase coherence of the condensate.

🎓 Theory — BCS Theory, Meissner Effect, and London Equations

Below the critical temperature Tc, electrons in certain materials form Cooper pairs — bosonic bound states of two electrons with opposite spin and momentum, glued together by the exchange of a lattice phonon. These pairs condense into a single macroscopic quantum state described by a wavefunction ψ = |ψ|eiφ. The condensate opens a forbidden energy gap 2Δ at the Fermi level, suppressing single-particle scattering and producing zero electrical resistance. BCS theory predicts the zero-temperature gap as:

$$\Delta(0) \approx 1.76\,k_B T_c$$

The Meissner effect — active expulsion of magnetic flux, B = 0 inside the bulk — distinguishes a superconductor from a mere perfect conductor and is captured by the London penetration depth λL. Type I and Type II superconductors differ in how they lose this state under applied field.

1. BCS Energy Gap

The superconducting gap Δ(T) opens below Tc and vanishes continuously at the transition. BCS theory gives the temperature dependence via the gap equation:

$$\frac{\Delta(T)}{\Delta(0)} \approx \tanh\!\left(1.74\sqrt{\frac{T_c}{T}-1}\right), \qquad \Delta(0) \approx 1.76\,k_B T_c$$

The full BCS gap equation is 1 = V·N(0)·∫0ℏωD dξ / √(ξ² + Δ²) · tanh(E/2kBT) with E = √(ξ² + Δ²). No single-particle states exist inside ±Δ; only Cooper-pair condensate and thermally excited quasiparticles live outside it.

2. Cooper Pairing

A passing electron attracts positive lattice ions, creating a transient wake of enhanced positive charge that pulls in a second electron. This phonon-mediated attraction overcomes Coulomb repulsion for electrons of opposite k and spin. The pair binding energy is tiny — about 2Δ ≈ 3.5 kBTc — yet because all pairs share the same quantum state, the condensate is rigid and coherent over macroscopic distances.

$$E_{\text{pair}} = 2\Delta, \qquad \xi_0 \approx \frac{\hbar v_F}{\pi \Delta(0)}$$

The coherence length ξ0 sets the spatial size of a pair. The isotope effect (Tc ∝ M−1/2) confirmed the phonon glue, since phonon frequencies scale as ωD ∝ M−1/2.

3. Meissner Effect

Cooled below Tc in a magnetic field, a superconductor expels all flux from its interior: perfect diamagnetism with susceptibility χ = −1 and B = 0 inside the bulk. This is an active thermodynamic response — shielding supercurrents circulate at the surface — not merely the freezing of pre-existing flux that a perfect conductor would do.

$$\chi = -1, \qquad \vec{B} = \vec{H} + \mu_0 \vec{M} = 0 \;\;\text{inside}$$

The Meissner effect proves superconductivity is a true thermodynamic phase, independent of the field history. The critical field Hc(T) = H0[1 − (T/Tc)²] marks where the condensation energy μ₀Hc²/2 can no longer sustain flux expulsion.

4. London Penetration Depth

The London equations describe how field decays over a characteristic length inside the superconductor. Field does not vanish instantly at the surface; it penetrates a thin shell of thickness λL:

$$\lambda_L = \sqrt{\frac{m}{\mu_0 n_s e^2}}, \qquad B(x) = B_0\, e^{-x/\lambda_L}$$

Here ns is the superfluid (Cooper-pair) density. As T → Tc, ns → 0 and λL → ∞, restoring full field penetration in the normal state. Typical values are tens to hundreds of nanometres.

5. Type I vs Type II Superconductors

Type I (e.g. Hg, Al, Pb) expel all field below a single Hc, then abruptly revert to normal. Type II (e.g. Nb, Nb₃Sn, YBCO) allow partial penetration via quantized Abrikosov vortices between a lower critical field Hc1 and an upper critical field Hc2:

$$H_{c1} < H < H_{c2}: \;\;\text{mixed state, } \Phi_0 = \frac{h}{2e}\;\text{per vortex}$$

Each vortex carries one flux quantum Φ0 = h/2e ≈ 2.07×10⁻¹⁵ Wb. Type II materials with high Hc2 (NbTi, Nb₃Sn) are the workhorses of high-field magnets for MRI, the LHC, and fusion reactors.
