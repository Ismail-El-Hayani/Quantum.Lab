Optics, Dispersion & Material Response

Explore how light interacts with matter across the optical spectrum. From Drude metals to semiconductors and insulators, observe how photon energy governs reflection, absorption, and transmission — and dive into the lattice to see why.

🧪 Material Preset

🔭 Macroscopic View — Photon Beam Interacting with Material

What you are seeing
A crystal lattice (structure & lattice parameter set by material) sits at the centre of the viewport. A laser on the left fires a photon packet (additive-glowing sphere) whose colour tracks photon energy: red (IR, ~1 eV) → yellow-green (visible, ~2.5 eV) → violet (UV, >3 eV).

• Incident — photon travels from source toward the lattice left face.
• Reflected — electrons screen the field (metals) or atoms elastically scatter (semiconductors); the packet bounces back.
• Refracted — below the bandgap / above the plasma edge the packet bends through via Snell’s law.
• Absorption glow — above-gap photons excite e–h pairs (semiconductors), excite the electron sea (metals), or launch phonon waves (insulators).
• Phonon wave — high-energy absorption deposits energy into quantized lattice vibrations (orange ring expanding).

Snell’s law: n₁ sin θ₁ = n(E) sin θ₂. Fresnel: reflectivity grows with angle and with k(E). Phonons: repeated absorption heats the lattice; atoms vibrate harder and bonds stretch until energy decays back to 300 K.
Photon color = energy (red→green→violet)

🔬 Microscopic View — What Happens at the Atomic Scale?

Expand mechanism by material type
Metal lattice (Cu, Ag, Al): Ion cores sit in a free-electron sea (cyan dots). Below the plasma edge, surface electrons screen the incoming E-field almost perfectly → total reflection. The reflected packet carries the incident colour because no energy is absorbed. Above the plasma edge, electrons can no longer follow the field → partial transmission. Absorption in metals heats the electron gas; electrons then thermalize via phonon scattering, raising lattice temperature.

Semiconductor lattice (Si, GaAs): Covalent bonds (grey cylinders) hold shared valence electrons (grey dots). Below the bandgap E < E_g there are no empty states to accept an electron → the lattice is transparent. Above the bandgap a photon breaks a bond: a valence electron jumps to the conduction band (cyan e⁻), leaving a hole behind (magenta h⁺). The e–h pair drifts apart; recombination later releases light (electroluminescence) or heat (phonons).

Insulator lattice (NaCl): Ionic crystal with alternating Na⁺ (orange) and Cl⁻ (blue) sites. The bandgap is enormous (>8 eV) so visible photons cannot excite electrons across it. At far-IR energies phonon modes resonate: the lattice absorbs photons as quantized vibrations. You see an expanding orange phonon wave ring. Energy is stored as lattice heat and gradually dissipates.

Damping γ = electron–phonon + impurity scattering rate. Interband transitions (Cu d-band → Fermi) add extra absorption in the red, giving copper its colour.

📈 Spectral Response — ε(ω), n, κ, R, T, A

What these curves show
The optical response of any material is governed by the complex dielectric function ε(ω) = ε₁(ω) + iε₂(ω), or equivalently the complex refractive index ñ = n + iκ where ε = ñ². n is the refractive index (bending / slowing of light); κ is the extinction coefficient (absorption / attenuation).

Drude metals: ε₁ < 0 below the plasma edge → imaginary refractive index → total reflection. Above Ep the metal becomes UV-transparent; damping γ broadens the step. Cu additionally has an interband Gaussian (d-band → Fermi) that steals blue/green light, producing its reddish hue.

NaCl: Lorentz phonon oscillator gives a sharp ε₂ peak near ωTO ≈ 0.02 eV. In the Reststrahlen band (ωTO < E < ωLO) ε₁ < 0 → high reflectivity and strong lattice absorption. This is the IR thermal-radiation behaviour of ionic crystals.

Semiconductors: Below Eg the material is transparent; above Eg interband excitations create strong absorption.

🔊 Phonon Excitation & Lattice Heating

Every absorbed photon dumps a fraction of its energy into phonons (quantized lattice vibrations). The phonon bath temperature rises following the Einstein model: T_lat = 300 K + c·E_phonon.

As T_lat grows, atoms oscillate with larger amplitude (Debye–Waller factor), and free electrons in metals scatter more strongly off the distorted lattice → resistivity rises. Phonons anharmonically decay over ~1 s in this toy model. Repeated high-energy shots heat the lattice; phonon energy dissipates gradually back to 300 K.

📐 Physical Motivation — Why does n depend on E?

The dielectric function ε(ω) = ε₁(ω) + iε₂(ω) encodes how the material polarizes under an oscillating electric field. For a free-electron metal:

ε₁(ω) = 1 − ωp² / (ω² + γ²)
ε₂(ω) = ωp² γ / (ω (ω² + γ²))

The plasma frequency ωp marks the boundary between metallic reflection and UV transparency. Damping γ = ħ/τ (scattering rate) broadens this edge. Real noble metals have low γ and stay brilliantly reflective; Al has higher damping and a softer edge.

For semiconductors and insulators, ε₂ ≠ 0 only when photons have enough energy to excite electrons across the bandgap Eg. Below Eg the material is transparent — the foundation of optical fibers and infrared windows.

🔗 Kramers–Kronig Causality

Because the response must be causal (effect never precedes cause), ε₁ and ε₂ are Hilbert-transform pairs. The UV absorption peak in ε₂ creates the rise in n(ω) (normal dispersion). Any region of strong absorption must be accompanied by anomalous dispersion nearby.

🔬 Try This

  • Set Al and sweep E from 10 eV to 20 eV — watch the reflected photon fade and the transmitted ray appear above the plasma edge.
  • Set NaCl and drop E below 0.1 eV — the IR phonon peak in ε₂ creates strong absorption bands responsible for thermal radiation behavior.
  • Compare Cu and Ag at 1 eV — both metals reflect, but Cu's interband absorption (d-band → Fermi) steals some red light, giving it a reddish hue.
