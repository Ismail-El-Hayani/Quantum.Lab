The Hydrogen Atom

The simplest atom reveals the deepest truths: orbitals, spectra, and the quantum leap.

📖 The Story — The Hydrogen Atom

For centuries, sunlight was simply light. Then, in 1814, Fraunhofer noticed dark lines cutting across the solar spectrum — signatures that the Sun itself was absorbing light at precise wavelengths. Decades later, Balmer found a simple formula for hydrogen's visible lines, but no one knew why it worked. Hydrogen, the simplest atom, became the Rosetta Stone of quantum mechanics.

🧪 Why It Matters

Hydrogen fuels stars, fills interstellar space, and gives us the 21 cm line that maps galaxies. Its spectrum calibrates telescopes, tests fundamental constants, and provides the energy-level template used to understand every other atom. Every semiconductor band diagram, every laser transition, every quantum dot begins with lessons learned from hydrogen.

🤯 What Surprprised Physicists

Classical electrons orbiting a nucleus should spiral inward and radiate within nanoseconds. Instead, hydrogen emits only specific colours — and its ground state is stable. Bohr patched the classical picture with ad-hoc quantization, but the real explanation came from Schrödinger's wave equation: electrons are not orbiting particles; they are stationary probability clouds described by quantum numbers \(n\), \(l\), and \(m\).

🎓 Theory — From the Bohr Model to the Schrödinger Atom

Hydrogen is special because its single electron moves in the exact \(1/r\) Coulomb potential of the proton. The Schrödinger equation separates into radial and angular parts, giving a complete set of orbitals labelled by three quantum numbers.

1. The Wavefunction

The full orbital is a product of a radial function \(R_{nl}(r)\) and a spherical harmonic \(Y_{lm}(\theta,\phi)\). The probability density of finding the electron at a point is the squared modulus of this product:

$$\psi_{{nlm}}(r, \theta, \phi) = R_{{nl}}(r)\, Y_{{lm}}(\theta, \phi), \qquad P(r,\theta,\phi) \propto |\psi_{{nlm}}|^2$$
2. Energy Levels

The Coulomb potential gives the famous Rydberg spectrum:

$$E_n = -\frac{{13.6\ \text{{eV}}}}{{n^2}}$$
3. Quantum Numbers

The integer labels emerge from boundary conditions on the wavefunction:

$$n = 1,2,3,... \quad l = 0,1,...,n-1 \quad m = -l,...,+l$$

\(n\) sets the energy and average size, \(l\) sets the orbital shape and number of radial nodes, and \(m\) sets the orientation in an external field.

4. The Rydberg Formula

Photons are emitted or absorbed when the electron jumps between shells:

$$\frac{{1}}{{\lambda}} = R_\infty \left(\frac{{1}}{{n_f^2}} - \frac{{1}}{{n_i^2}}\right)$$
5. Zeeman Splitting

In a weak magnetic field, each orbital level with angular momentum \(l\) splits into \(2l+1\) sub-levels:

$$\Delta E = \mu_B B, \qquad \mu_B = \frac{{e\hbar}}{{2m_e}} \approx 5.788 \times 10^{{-5}}\ \text{{eV/T}}$$

📡 Emission Spectrum

Why are hydrogen lines discrete?
Bound electron energies are quantized as En = −13.6 eV / n². A photon emitted in a transition ni → nf carries exactly the energy difference, so only specific wavelengths appear. The series are named by the final level: Lyman (nf=1, UV), Balmer (nf=2, visible), Paschen (nf=3, IR), and Brackett (nf=4, IR).

How do I read this plot?
Each coloured dot is one possible electron transition in hydrogen. The x-axis shows the photon energy released (eV). The y-axis groups transitions by their final level — the series they belong to. The dashed vertical line marks your chosen ni → nf transition.
What are the rules?
  1. Energy is quantized: En = −13.6 / n² eV.
  2. A photon carries exactly the energy gap: Ephoton = Eni − Enf.
  3. The series is named by nf: Lyman (1), Balmer (2), Paschen (3), Brackett (4).
  4. Longer jumps (higher ni) give higher-energy photons → UV/blue.
  5. Shorter jumps give lower-energy photons → red/IR.
📐 Rule of the Plot

The spectrum plot visualises the Rydberg formula. Every dot represents a unique transition. The colour encodes the series (final level), while the x-position encodes the photon energy. The dashed line shows your selected transition. When you change ni or nf, the dashed line moves to the new photon energy — if it lands on an existing dot, that transition is physically allowed.

Ephoton = 13.6 (1/nf² − 1/ni²) eV

Tip: Start with Balmer (nf=2). All visible light from hydrogen comes from this series. Lyman lines are UV (invisible), Paschen and Brackett are infrared.

🧲 Zeeman Splitting

What is the normal Zeeman effect?
In an external magnetic field B, the magnetic quantum number m lifts the degeneracy of a level with angular momentum l. The level splits into 2l + 1 sub-levels with energies E = En + m μB B. Adjacent levels are separated by the same energy μB B.
Zeeman Splitting E = En + m μBB

What is plotted?

Energy levels for each magnetic sub-state m = −l, ..., +l. Adjacent levels are separated by μBB.

✨ Why does this matter?

Zeeman splitting revealed that the electron carries an intrinsic magnetic moment. It underpins magnetic resonance imaging, atomic clocks, and the fine structure of spectral lines. The splitting is tiny for hydrogen in everyday fields, but it is the foundation of quantum magnetism.

🏎 Challenge 3: Bohr Radius Scaling Hard
The Bohr radius scales as ⟨r⟩ ∝ n². If the ground state (n=1) has radius ~1.5 a₀, how many times larger is the n=3 state?
🏆 Reward: 100 XP + "Bohr Master" badge
What it shows: The Bohr radius scales as ⟨r⟩ = a₀ n² [1 + ½(1 − l(l+1)/n²)], so for any fixed l the dominant dependence is n². The n=3 state is therefore 9× larger than n=1 in spatial extent — a direct consequence of the Coulomb potential's 1/r form and the virial theorem. This scaling controls how tightly bound an electron is and therefore how strongly it interacts with external fields.
