Junctions & Devices

Engineer p-n junctions, master diode rectification, and unlock solar-cell efficiency by controlling doping, depletion, and built-in potential.

📖 The Story — Junctions That Built the Information Age

In 1949, William Shockley published the p-n junction theory — the mathematical foundation of the modern semiconductor era. The key idea: when p-type and n-type materials meet, diffusion of holes and electrons creates a charge-depleted region that builds an internal electric field. This built-in potential can be lowered by forward bias (current flows) or raised by reverse bias (current blocks). This simple asymmetry is the heart of every diode, solar cell, LED, and — by extension — every transistor.

🧩 The p-n Junction

A p-n junction is the boundary where acceptor-doped (p) and donor-doped (n) regions meet. Holes diffuse from p to n, electrons from n to p, leaving behind fixed ionised impurities that create the depletion region. The built-in field opposes further diffusion — equilibrium is reached when the diffusion current and drift current exactly cancel.

🔬 From Diode to MOSFET

The MOSFET (Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor Field-Effect Transistor) replaces the junction with a gate-controlled channel. A voltage on the gate attracts carriers to the oxide interface, forming an inversion layer that conducts between source and drain. The ability to switch this channel on and off with zero static power (CMOS) is why modern chips pack billions of transistors.

🎓 Theory — Junction Physics, Diode Equation, and MOSFET

The built-in potential of a p-n junction arises from the difference in Fermi levels between the p- and n-type regions at equilibrium:

$$V_{bi} = \frac{k_B T}{e}\ln\left(\frac{N_a N_d}{n_i^2}\right)$$

The depletion width depends on the applied bias:

$$W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s}{e}\left(\frac{1}{N_a}+\frac{1}{N_d}\right)(V_{bi}-V)}$$

Under forward bias (\(V > 0\)) the barrier is reduced; under reverse bias (\(V < 0\)) it is increased. The depletion region extends farther into the lightly doped side.

1. Ideal Diode Equation

The Shockley ideal diode law describes the current through a p-n junction:

$$I = I_0\left(e^{eV/nk_B T}-1\right)$$

where \(I_0\) is the saturation current and \(n\) is the ideality factor (1 for ideal diffusion current, ~2 when recombination dominates). With photocurrent \(I_{ph}\), the same device becomes a solar cell: \(I = I_0(e^{eV/nkT} - 1) - I_{ph}\).

2. Breakdown Mechanisms

Zener breakdown (~3-6 V): band-to-band tunneling in heavily doped junctions. The depletion is so narrow that electrons tunnel quantum-mechanically through the barrier.
Avalanche breakdown (>6 V): impact ionisation in the depletion region — high-energy carriers create electron-hole pairs, causing a multiplicative cascade.

3. Tunnel Diode (Esaki Diode)

Ultra-heavy doping (\(\sim 10^{19}\) cm\(^{-3}\)) makes the depletion region so thin that quantum tunneling dominates. The I-V curve shows a negative differential resistance (NDR) region — current decreases as voltage increases — enabling high-frequency oscillators and ultra-fast logic.

4. MOSFET — Gate-Controlled Transport

In a MOSFET, the gate voltage controls the channel conductivity. The threshold voltage \(V_{th}\) marks the onset of inversion. The drain current in the triode region (linear) and saturation region:

$$I_D = \mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}\left[(V_{GS}-V_{th})V_{DS}-\frac{V_{DS}^2}{2}\right] \quad\text{(triode)}$$ $$I_D = \frac{\mu_n C_{ox}}{2}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_{th})^2 \quad\text{(saturation)}$$

CMOS pairs n-MOS and p-MOS so only one device conducts at any time — the reason modern chips consume negligible static power.
