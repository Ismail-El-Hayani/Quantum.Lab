Magnetism

From dia- to ferromagnetism: how magnetic moments respond, interact, and order.

📖 The Story of Magnetism

Magnetism has been known since antiquity — naturally magnetized lodestone (Fe₃O₄) was used as a compass by the Han dynasty (206 BC). In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted discovered that an electric current deflects a compass needle, linking electricity and magnetism. Michael Faraday (1831) showed that a changing magnetic field induces an electric current — the principle behind generators and transformers. James Clerk Maxwell (1865) unified electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single theory of electromagnetism.

The quantum understanding emerged with the Pauli exclusion principle (1925), Heisenberg exchange (1928), and Louis Néel's theory of antiferromagnetism (1936). Nobel Prizes: Fert & Grünberg (2007) for giant magnetoresistance (GMR) — the foundation of modern hard-drive read heads; and the 2023 prize to L'Huillier, Corkum & Krausz for attosecond physics, which now probes magnetic switching on femtosecond timescales.

🧲 Types of Magnetism

Diamagnetism — χ < 0, weak, present in all materials. Arises from induced orbital currents opposing the applied field. Examples: water (χ ≈ −9×10⁻⁶), graphite, copper, superconductors (χ = −1, perfect diamagnet).

Paramagnetism — χ > 0, χ ∝ 1/T (Curie law). Independent atomic moments align with the field against thermal disorder. Examples: Al, Pt, O₂ gas, rare-earth ions. Pauli paramagnetism in metals: χ is T-independent because only electrons near EF respond.

Ferromagnetism — spontaneous magnetization below Tc. Exchange interaction J > 0 aligns neighbouring spins. Domains form to reduce stray field energy. Examples: Fe (Tc=1043 K), Ni (627 K), Co (1388 K), Gd (293 K).

Antiferromagnetism — J < 0, spins alternate (checkerboard). Zero net M but nonzero sublattice magnetization. Peaks in χ at TN. Examples: Cr (TN=311 K), MnO (116 K), NiO (525 K).

Ferrimagnetism — antiparallel sublattices with unequal moments, giving net spontaneous M. Examples: Fe₃O₄ magnetite (Tc=858 K), YIG (yttrium iron garnet), ferrites used in transformers.

⚡ Everyday Applications

Hard disk drives store bits as magnetic domains in CoCrPt thin films. The read head uses GMR: resistance changes by 5–20% when the sense-layer magnetization switches relative to a pinned reference layer. MRI (3 T clinical scanners) uses the Larmor precession of proton spins in water — the same physics as the Bloch sphere in Module 3. Transformers use laminated ferrite cores (soft magnets, low Hc) to efficiently couple AC power. Spintronics exploits the electron's spin degree of freedom beyond charge: spin-transfer torque, magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJ), and racetrack memory are the frontier.

🔭 Open Questions

Why do some 2D materials (CrI₃, Cr₂Ge₂Te₆) remain ferromagnetic down to monolayer thickness? Can we build an antiskyrmion-based racetrack memory? What is the role of spin-orbit coupling in topological magnets like MnSi and Co₃Sn₂S₂? How does ultrafast demagnetization work — electrons, spins, and lattice exchange energy within 100 fs after a laser pulse? These are the questions driving modern magnetism research.

🔬 Curie-Weiss Theory

Pierre Curie (1895) observed that paramagnetic susceptibility follows χ = C/T. Pierre Weiss (1907) introduced a molecular field Hm = λM to explain ferromagnetism: each spin feels an effective field from its neighbours. The susceptibility becomes:

$$\chi = \frac{C}{T - \theta}, \qquad \theta = \lambda C, \qquad M_s(T) \propto \sqrt{T_c - T} \quad (\text{mean-field})$$

θ > 0 for ferromagnets (Tc ≈ θ), θ < 0 for antiferromagnets (TN = −θ).

🔗 Heisenberg Exchange

The quantum origin of the molecular field is the exchange interaction: the Coulomb repulsion combined with the Pauli principle favours parallel (ferro) or antiparallel (antiferro) alignment depending on orbital overlap.

$$\mathcal{H}_{\text{Heis}} = -J \sum_{\langle ij \rangle} \mathbf{S}_i \cdot \mathbf{S}_j, \qquad J = \frac{1}{2}(E_{\text{singlet}} - E_{\text{triplet}})$$

J > 0 → ferromagnetic (triplet lower energy), J < 0 → antiferromagnetic (singlet lower). The mean-field transition temperature scales as Tc ≈ z|J|/kB where z is the coordination number.

📉 Landau Theory of Phase Transitions

Lev Landau (1937) described continuous phase transitions using a free-energy functional expanded in powers of the order parameter M:

$$G(M) = a(T - T_c) M^2 + b M^4 - \mu_0 H M, \qquad \frac{\partial G}{\partial M} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; M(T) \propto \sqrt{T_c - T}$$

Below Tc the quadratic coefficient becomes negative, producing a double-well potential. The system spontaneously chooses M = ±Ms — symmetry breaking. Adding the −HM term tilts the well, favouring one direction.

🏛 Domains & Hysteresis

A ferromagnet below Tc breaks into magnetic domains — regions of uniform magnetization — to minimize the magnetostatic (stray field) energy. Domain walls (Bloch or Néel type) have a finite width set by the competition between exchange (wants wide walls) and anisotropy (wants narrow walls).

Hysteresis arises because domain walls are pinned by defects, grain boundaries, or inclusions. The coercivity Hc is the field needed to unpin walls and reverse magnetization. Soft magnets (Hc < 1 kA/m) are used in transformers; hard magnets (Hc > 10 kA/m) are permanent magnets.

$$M(H) = M_s \tanh\!\left(\frac{H \pm H_c}{\alpha}\right), \qquad H_c(T) = H_c(0)\left[1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{1/2}\right]$$

🧊 Magnons (Spin Waves)

At low temperature, the ordered magnet supports collective excitations called magnons — quantized spin waves. In a simple cubic ferromagnet with nearest-neighbour exchange:

$$\hbar\omega(q) = 2JS(1 - \cos qa), \qquad \text{low-}q:\; \hbar\omega \approx JS(qa)^2$$

Magnons carry spin −ℏ and reduce the magnetization at finite T: the Bloch T3/2 law. In the Magnon sub-tab of the Playground, you can inject a magnon wavepacket into the Ising lattice and watch it propagate — a real-space visualization of spin-wave dynamics.

📈 Magnetic Susceptibility χ(T)

Curie-Weiss: χ = C/(T−θ). Peaks at Néel temperature for antiferromagnets.

What it shows: χ(T) distinguising paramagnetic Curie-law decay (χ = C/T), ferromagnetic Curie–Weiss divergence near Tc (χ = C/(T−θ)), and the antiferromagnetic peak at TN.

Why it matters: The χ(T) fingerprint tells you the dominant interaction — weak independent moments vs strong exchange — and predicts phase transitions.

Spontaneous Ms(T) ∝ √(Tc−T) in mean-field.

What it shows: Normalized M(T)/Ms vs T. Below Tc, exchange wins over thermal disorder.

Why it matters: The M(T) knee is the smoking gun for a ferromagnetic transition.

📈 Hysteresis Loop M(H)

Coercivity Hc, remanence Mr, saturation Ms.

What it shows: Classic M–H loop: sweep H up (green) and down (magenta). As T → Tc the loop collapses.

Why it matters: Loop area and coercivity decide soft vs hard magnet — transformer core vs permanent magnet.

✨ Why does this matter?

In paramagnets, moments align against thermal disorder (χ ∝ 1/T). In ferromagnets, exchange produces spontaneous order below Tc. Domains are the microscopic consequence: locally aligned regions that can cancel globally until a field aligns them. The hysteresis loop encodes memory: coercivity, remanence, and saturation determine whether a material is soft or hard.

$$\chi = \frac{C}{T - \theta}, \qquad M \propto \tanh\left(\frac{\mu H}{k_B T}\right), \qquad B = \mu_0(H + M)$$

📐 Landau Free Energy G(M)

G(M) = a(T − Tc)M² + bM⁴ − HM. Below Tc the quadratic coefficient becomes negative → double-well → spontaneous symmetry breaking.

What it shows: The Landau free-energy functional. At T < Tc the double minima at ±Ms represent the two degenerate ferromagnetic ground states. External field H tilts the well, favouring one orientation. The barrier between minima determines how stable domains are against thermal fluctuations.

Why it matters: Landau theory is the universal framework for continuous phase transitions — used for superconductivity (Ginzburg-Landau), ferroelectrics, liquid crystals, and the Higgs mechanism.

$$G(M) = a(T - T_c)M^2 + bM^4 - \mu_0 H M, \qquad \frac{\partial G}{\partial M} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; M \propto \sqrt{T_c - T}$$

📈 Magnon Dispersion ω(q)

ℏω(q) = 2JS(1 − cos qa). Low‑q: ω ∝ q² (Goldstone mode). Zone boundary: ω = 4JS.

What it shows: The magnon dispersion for a nearest-neighbour Heisenberg ferromagnet on a square lattice. The quadratic low‑q behaviour is characteristic of ferromagnets (antiferromagnets have ω ∝ q). The exchange coupling J (sidebar) directly scales the energy axis — higher J = stiffer spin waves = faster propagation.

Why it matters: Magnon spectra measured by inelastic neutron scattering directly reveal J. The T3/2 Bloch law for M(T) follows from integrating the magnon density of states.
